# The Masters

Excerpts from PG, Naval, Bacon, and Orwell, annotated for what makes each work. Use these as anchors when diagnosing and teaching.

## Paul Graham

### From "Write Simply" (2021)

> I try to write using ordinary words and simple sentences.
>
> That kind of writing is easier to read, and the easier something is to read, the more deeply readers will engage with it. The less energy they expend on your prose, the more they'll have left for your ideas.

**What makes it work:** The first sentence is a declaration, not a setup. No "in this essay I will argue." He just says it. The second sentence builds a causal chain with three plain words: "easier," "easier," "more deeply." No Latinate haze. The principle and the prose match.

> When I write a sentence that seems too complicated, or that uses unnecessarily intellectual words, it doesn't seem fancy to me. It seems clumsy.

**What makes it work:** A reframe without argument. He doesn't say "fancy writing is bad." He shows what it looks like to him. The aesthetic claim does the work the moral claim would have done — better, because it's harder to argue with.

### From "How to Write Usefully" (2020)

> Useful writing makes claims that are as strong as they can be made without becoming false.

**What makes it work:** The whole essay's thesis in one sentence. No qualifications. The construction "as strong as they can be made without becoming false" is itself an example of the rule — pushed to the edge.

> Brevity is the diction of command. If you watch someone delivering unwelcome news from a position of inferiority, you'll notice they tend to use lots of words, to soften the blow.

**What makes it work:** A fresh metaphor ("diction of command") then a concrete observation that earns it. He doesn't say "be brief"; he shows what brevity *is*.

### PG's rules (synthesized)

- Write the first draft fast. Then spend days editing.
- Don't publish bad sentences. Delete them and try again.
- A sentence should sound the way you'd say it to a friend.
- Strong claim + precise qualification > weak claim with no qualification.
- If you can't get it right, don't publish it at all. You don't have a deadline.

## Naval Ravikant

### From "How to Get Rich (without getting lucky)"

> Seek wealth, not money or status.
>
> Wealth is having assets that earn while you sleep.

**What makes it work:** Two sentences, two definitions, zero throat-clearing. The second sentence is the proof of the first — you cannot dispute the rule once you accept the definition. Naval is an aphorist who hides arguments inside definitions.

> You're not going to get rich renting out your time.
>
> You must own equity — a piece of a business — to gain your financial freedom.

**What makes it work:** Negative claim, then positive claim. The dash insertion ("— a piece of a business —") is the only ornament. Everything else is steel. This is the parallelism Bacon uses, four hundred years later.

> Escape competition through authenticity.

**What makes it work:** A complete idea in four words. Verb-first. The whole argument compressed into a mnemonic. This is the limit case of "value per word."

### Naval's pattern

- Each unit (sentence, tweet) is standalone.
- Definitions do the persuasion. State the definition crisply enough and the conclusion is inescapable.
- Parallelism for compression: "Wealth is X. Money is Y. Status is Z."
- Verbs first. Imperatives. Aphorisms.
- He rewrites the same idea ten times until it's four words. Most of what reaches the page is the survivor of that cull.

## Francis Bacon

### From "Of Studies" (1625)

> Studies serve for delight, for ornament, and for ability.

**What makes it work:** Six words. Three uses, parallel construction, no connectives wasted. The whole essay's frame in the first sentence. This is the move Naval would make 400 years later.

> Some books are to be tasted, others to be swallowed, and some few to be chewed and digested.

**What makes it work:** A single fresh metaphor (books as food) extended through three verbs. The verbs do the work — taste, swallow, chew. No abstraction. You can *see* it.

> Reading maketh a full man; conference a ready man; and writing an exact man.

**What makes it work:** Triplet parallelism, each clause shedding the connective. The third clause drops "maketh"; the reader supplies it. This is how compression *uses* the reader's mind to do work.

> Histories make men wise; poets witty; the mathematics subtile; natural philosophy deep; moral grave; logic and rhetoric able to contend.

**What makes it work:** Six pairings in one sentence. Each pair is two or three words. No connective tissue. Bacon is showing you that argument can be a list of crystals.

### Bacon's pattern

- Aphoristic compression. Each sentence is a finished gem.
- Parallelism is the engine: "X is A. Y is B. Z is C."
- Heavy use of monosyllabic Saxon verbs (maketh, serve, read, chew) inside Latinate frames.
- Drops connectives. Trusts the reader to bridge.
- Treats the essay as a series of claims, not a developing argument.

## George Orwell

### From "Politics and the English Language" (1946)

> The great enemy of clear language is insincerity.

**What makes it work:** Diagnosis in one line. No examples yet; the claim stands alone. Then the rest of the essay earns it.

> When there is a gap between one's real and one's declared aims, one turns as it were instinctively to long words and exhausted idioms, like a cuttlefish spurting out ink.

**What makes it work:** The cuttlefish metaphor is fresh, concrete, and biologically precise. It's exactly the kind of metaphor Orwell tells you to use: invented for the occasion, evoking a picture.

> A scrupulous writer, in every sentence that he writes, will ask himself at least four questions, thus: What am I trying to say? What words will express it? What image or idiom will make it clearer? Is this image fresh enough to have an effect? And he will probably ask himself two more: Could I put it more shortly? Have I said anything that is avoidably ugly?

**The six questions.** Memorize these. Apply them sentence by sentence.

### Orwell's six rules

i. Never use a metaphor, simile or other figure of speech which you are used to seeing in print.
ii. Never use a long word where a short one will do.
iii. If it is possible to cut a word out, always cut it out.
iv. Never use the passive where you can use the active.
v. Never use a foreign phrase, a scientific word or a jargon word if you can think of an everyday English equivalent.
vi. Break any of these rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.

### Orwell's diagnostic categories

1. **Dying metaphors** — phrases that were once vivid and are now exhausted. The writer uses them to save the trouble of thinking.
2. **Operators / verbal false limbs** — multi-word constructions where a single verb would do. "Render inoperative" for "break."
3. **Pretentious diction** — Latinate or technical words used to dress up simple statements.
4. **Meaningless words** — abstract words that no longer point to anything. "Democracy," "freedom," "values" used as warm noise.

### The Ecclesiastes test

Orwell's most useful demonstration: take a sentence in clear English, then translate it into bad modern English, and watch what is lost.

> Original: "I returned and saw under the sun, that the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong… but time and chance happeneth to them all."
>
> Modernized: "Objective consideration of contemporary phenomena compels the conclusion that success or failure in competitive activities exhibits no tendency to be commensurate with innate capacity, but that a considerable element of the unpredictable must invariably be taken into account."

The original is concrete: race, battle, bread. The translation is abstract: "competitive activities." The original has rhythm; the translation has syllables. Apply this test to suspect prose: can you swap each abstract noun for a picture?

## The shared DNA

All four converge on the same moves, separated by centuries:

1. **Concrete over abstract.** Picture before word.
2. **Short over long.** Cut, then cut again.
3. **Active over passive.** Name the actor.
4. **Fresh metaphor or none.** Stale figures signal stale thought.
5. **Strong claim, calibrated qualification.** Push to the edge of true.
6. **One unit, one idea.** A sentence is a load-bearing beam, not a freight train.
7. **Parallel structures compress.** "X is A. Y is B."
8. **Honesty as discipline.** You cannot write clearly about something you do not understand.

The eighth is the deepest. Orwell's whole essay is built on it. PG and Naval both arrive there independently. Bacon assumes it.
