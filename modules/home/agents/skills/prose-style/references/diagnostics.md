# Diagnostics, expanded

Use this when running a deep check or teaching a specific principle. Each diagnostic includes detection patterns, examples drawn from common writing genres, common edge cases, and the cure.

## 1. Borrowed thinking

### Detection patterns
- Business jargon: "move the needle," "circle back," "ladder up to," "lean in," "double-click on," "synergy," "alignment," "north star."
- Startup jargon: "10x," "moat," "thesis," "asymmetric bet" (when used vaguely), "stack."
- Academic jargon: "interrogate," "problematize," "unpack," "lens," "discourse."
- Inherited metaphors: "tip of the iceberg," "low-hanging fruit," "skin in the game" (Taleb's; fine if you're using it precisely, but often borrowed), "Achilles' heel," "the elephant in the room."
- Stale similes: "like a well-oiled machine," "like a deer in headlights."

### Why it's a thinking failure
Each of these phrases came pre-assembled. The user didn't choose them word by word. Orwell's diagnosis: the writer reached for a phrase to save the trouble of inventing one. The cost is that you don't know whether the phrase is accurate. "Low-hanging fruit" sounds easy. Are you actually picking the easy thing, or just the closest? The borrowed phrase hides the question.

### Cure
Two options:
1. **Invent a fresh image.** Orwell does this himself: insincerity is "like a cuttlefish spurting out ink." Naval: wealth is "the factory of robots cranking out things."
2. **Drop the figure entirely.** State the thing literally. "We should start with the easiest customers" beats "let's pick the low-hanging fruit."

### Edge case
Some metaphors are dead — fully reverted to ordinary words. "Iron resolution" is dead; nobody pictures iron. These are fine. The danger zone is the middle: metaphors with just enough life that they create the *illusion* of vividness while delivering none. That middle zone is what Orwell calls "dying metaphors." If the phrase is the first one that came to mind, suspect it.

## 2. Abstraction haze

### Detection patterns
- Latinate verbs: utilize, facilitate, optimize, implement, leverage, enable, operationalize, incentivize, instantiate.
- Nominalizations: "the implementation of," "the optimization of," "the deployment of," "the consideration of," "the discussion of." (Convert verbs to nouns to dodge committing to who did what when.)
- Vague abstract nouns: factors, aspects, considerations, dynamics, processes, elements, components, mechanisms.
- Bureaucratic adjectives: strategic, comprehensive, holistic, integrated, robust, scalable.

### Why it's a thinking failure
The user wrote words about the topic without picturing the topic. The prose is *about* something rather than *of* something. Orwell: "Probably it is better to put off using words as long as possible and get one's meanings as clear as one can through pictures and sensations."

### The Ecclesiastes test
Take a suspect sentence. Can you replace each abstract noun with a picture? If not, the user hasn't seen what they're describing.

Example:
> "Our strategic initiative will leverage cross-functional alignment to drive optimal outcomes."

Run the test: What's the strategic initiative? Picture it. What does "leverage cross-functional alignment" look like — three people in a meeting? What's the outcome? You can't picture any of it. The sentence is structurally hollow.

Rewrite (once you find out what they mean):
> "We're going to ask engineering and sales to sit in the same meeting once a week, so we stop shipping things nobody can sell."

### Cure
1. Picture the actual thing.
2. Name it with concrete nouns and active verbs.
3. If you can't picture it, you don't yet know what you mean. Stop writing and think.

## 3. Hedge cloud

### Detection patterns
- Stacked qualifiers: "I think it might possibly be that," "it could perhaps be argued," "in some sense it may be the case."
- Phantom-attribution hedges: "some might say," "one could argue," "there's a view that" — when the user *is* the one saying it.
- Closing hedges: "but of course this is just my opinion," "though there are many perspectives on this."
- The aggregate effect: three or more hedges around a single claim.

### Why it's a thinking failure
PG: qualifications are not weakness — they're calibration. The problem is *over-qualification*. The user hasn't decided how confident they are, so they reach for cotton. Or they have decided and are afraid the reader will object. Either way, the claim doesn't reach the reader with its weight intact.

### Cure
PG's two-step:
1. Decide your actual confidence level.
2. Use *one* qualifier that precisely expresses it. "Usually" / "in my experience" / "for software companies, at least" / "between 2015 and 2022." Each carries a real meaning. "Sort of perhaps maybe possibly" carries none.

Example:
> "I think it might be the case that, in some sense, founders tend to be slightly overoptimistic about their timelines."

Cure:
> "Founders are overoptimistic about their timelines."

Or, if you want to keep one calibration:
> "Founders are usually overoptimistic about their timelines."

The first cure is bolder. PG would prefer it. The second is safer. Either beats the original.

### Edge case
Sometimes hedging is genuine. If you really don't know, say so cleanly: "I'm not sure, but I suspect X." That single sentence is honest. The disease is hedge *clouds*, not hedges per se.

## 4. Throat-clearing

### Detection patterns
- Meta-announcements: "In this post I'll discuss…" "Let me start by saying…" "I want to begin with…"
- Setup paragraphs that don't deliver content: an entire first paragraph explaining why the topic matters, before saying anything about the topic.
- Recapping what the reader just read: "As we've discussed above…" (more than once or twice).
- Signaling the structure: "First I'll cover X, then Y, then Z."

### Why it's a thinking failure
The user doesn't know where to start, so they describe starting. Or they're scared to commit to the first real claim, so they orbit it. The reader pays for this in attention they will not get back.

### Cure
Delete every sentence before the first real claim. Almost always, the essay begins three paragraphs in. Cut to there.

Example:
> "Hiring is one of the most important topics in startups. Everyone has opinions about it. In this post I want to share some thoughts on what I've learned from hiring at three different companies. Let me start by saying that there's no one-size-fits-all approach. With that caveat in mind, here's the most important lesson I've learned: don't hire anyone you wouldn't enjoy having a long lunch with."

The whole post is in the last sentence. Cut everything before it.

### Edge case
Sometimes a one-sentence frame helps. "Most hiring advice is wrong because it focuses on filters instead of attractors." That's a frame, not throat-clearing — it makes a claim. The test: does the sentence advance the argument, or describe that an argument is about to start?

## 5. Weak claim

### Detection patterns
- Universal hedges that survive any counterexample: "Many factors influence X." "Communication is important." "It depends."
- Truisms presented as insights: "People are complicated." "There are tradeoffs."
- Both-sides framings with no resolution: "Some say A, others say B. The truth is probably somewhere in between."

### Why it's a thinking failure
PG: "It's easy to make a statement correct by making it vague. That's a common flaw in academic writing." The user retreated to a defensible position. They prioritized being unarguable over being useful. The result is a sentence that says nothing.

### Cure
Push to the edge. Find what's specifically true. Replace the vague universal with a precise edge.

Examples:
- "Many factors influence startup success" → "Startup success is mostly about hiring. The product matters less than people think; the timing matters more."
- "Communication is important in relationships" → "Most relationships die from accumulated unspoken complaints."
- "It depends" → "It depends on whether you're optimizing for revenue this year or revenue in five years. If revenue this year, charge more. If revenue in five years, charge less."

### The strength test
PG: "Make claims that are as strong as they can be made without becoming false." For each weak claim, ask: what's the strongest version that's still true? Write that.

## 6. Hidden actor

### Detection patterns
- Bare passive: "Mistakes were made." "It was decided that."
- Agentless transformation: "Users are shown a notification." (Shown by whom? The system? Your code? The PM who specced it?)
- Process-disguised-as-actor: "The system handles this" when it's actually a person making a choice.

### Why it's a thinking failure
The user removed the subject from the sentence — usually because committing to the subject would require committing to responsibility. "Mistakes were made" hides who made them. "It was decided" hides who decided.

### Cure
Name the actor. If you can't, ask why. Sometimes the answer is honest (the actor genuinely doesn't matter; the process does). Often the answer is evasion (you don't want to attribute the action to anyone, including yourself).

Example:
> "It was determined that the project would be sunsetted, and users would be notified accordingly."

Cure:
> "I shut down the project. We told users on Tuesday."

The cured version is shorter, clearer, and committed. The original was longer, vaguer, and evasive.

### Edge case
Passive is sometimes correct. "The vase was broken" is right if you don't know who broke it, or if the breakage matters more than the breaker. The diagnostic fires on *agentless passive used to evade*, not on passive in general.

## 7. Verbal false limbs

### Detection patterns
Direct from Orwell:
- "Render inoperative" → break
- "Militate against" → work against
- "Prove unacceptable" → not work
- "Make contact with" → meet, call
- "Be subject to" → undergo
- "Give rise to" → cause
- "Have the effect of" → cause
- "Exhibit a tendency to" → tend to
- "Be in a position to" → can
- "Have a discussion about" → discuss
- "Take action on" → act on
- "Reach a decision" → decide

### Why it's a thinking failure
Each false limb is a phrase doing the work of one verb. The user reached for the phrase reflexively. The cost is extra syllables and reduced precision.

### Cure
Replace with the simple verb. Always. There is no edge case.

## 8. One sentence, multiple weights

### Detection patterns
- Multi-clause sentences carrying three or more ideas.
- Sentences with parenthetical asides that contain their own claims.
- Sentences over 40 words doing more than introducing a complex idea.

### Why it's a thinking failure
The user didn't separate their thoughts. They wrote them in the order they arrived, glued together. The reader has to disassemble the sentence to find the ideas, which costs attention.

### Cure
One sentence, one weight. Break into multiple sentences. Each carries one idea.

Example (44 words, three ideas):
> "While it's true that early-stage startups should focus on growth (which is what most VCs care about, although there are exceptions), it's also important to recognize that growth without retention is essentially burning money, and many founders fall into this trap."

Cure (three sentences, three ideas):
> "Early-stage startups should focus on growth. But growth without retention burns money. Most founders learn this too late."

The cured version is shorter, has a clearer beat, and exposes that the third sentence is the actual claim. The original buried the point in its own apparatus.

### Edge case
Some ideas genuinely need a long sentence — a complex causal chain, a series of conditions. Long sentences are fine when each clause does load-bearing work. The diagnostic fires on *sentences carrying multiple separable ideas*, not on length alone.

## Genre-specific patterns

### Technical writing
Worst offenders: nominalizations ("the implementation of"), passive voice ("the function is called"), abstract nouns ("the system handles state"). Cure: pick verbs, name the actor, picture the actual data moving.

### Marketing copy / landing pages
Worst offenders: borrowed thinking (every B2B cliché), abstraction haze ("scalable, holistic solutions"), weak claims ("we help you achieve your goals"). Cure: name what you specifically do, for whom, with what specific result.

### Academic / analytical writing
Worst offenders: hedge clouds, throat-clearing, "it could be argued." Cure: commit. If you're not sure, say so cleanly. If you are sure, say it.

### Tweets / short posts
Worst offenders: trying to fit three ideas into one tweet (the "one sentence, multiple weights" pattern at smaller scale). Cure: one tweet, one idea. Either thread or cut.

### Founder updates / memos
Worst offenders: hidden actor, throat-clearing, weak claims to manage expectations. Cure: name what happened, who did it, what you'll do next.

## A note on personal voice

The skill should not flatten every writer into a Naval clone. PG, Naval, Bacon, and Orwell share principles but sound different. PG is conversational; Naval is aphoristic; Bacon is courtly; Orwell is political. The principles are the floor, not the ceiling. After mastering the principles, the user should sound like themselves — only sharper.
